Claude finds ART: CRISPR-like enzyme, function still unknown

What happened

Anthropic’s new life-sciences lab says Claude autonomously spotted a previously uncharacterized enzyme system in bacteriophage DNA — array-associated reverse transcriptases (ART) — with a CRISPR-like tandem-repeat layout. The company posted the write-up Wednesday (Sep 23) and a preprint; major press picked it up into Thursday.

The setup: high-level human prompt, then roughly 950 Claude agents for 21 hours (~210 million tokens) mining reverse-transcriptase loci across a huge sequence store (preprint: ~1.9 billion protein clusters). Agents gathered 200k+ RTs, cut to ~3,500 odd candidates, then ~20 reports for humans. One agent, reading raw DNA next to an odd RT, flagged a tandem repeat array nobody had named as a system.

ART has three parts: the RT (known in jumbo phages, not as this package), a partner gene of unknown function, and a long evenly spaced DNA-repeat array. Early lab work shows the array is expressed as distinct short RNAs — the kind of layout that makes CRISPR programmable. Anthropic is clear: primary function is still unknown. No claim it cuts, edits, or cures anything yet.

Safety

This is the same week labs are arguing about bio/cyber brakes on frontier models. Anthropic’s Bay Area bench is BSL-1 / BSL-2 only — no human-infecting pathogens. Wet-lab work stays with human scientists; Claude searched, hypothesized, and wrote reports. That line matters for dual-use: agent-scale genome mining can surface programmable-looking systems faster than a lone grad student, even when the “next CRISPR” claim is not proven.

Outside pushback is already public. Microbiologist Kevin Blake told Al Jazeera that CRISPR-like repeats in nature are common and that nothing here shows a rival to CRISPR-the-therapy. Follower hype is not evidence. Feng Zhang (MIT / Broad, CRISPR pioneer) called the RNA-repeat + RT link “genuinely intriguing” and worth more work — endorsement of the anomaly, not of a product.

Use case

Named real workflow, not vapor: Anthropic’s life-sciences group (stood up spring 2026) ran the campaign in Claude Science and Claude Code — the same tools they say any scientist can use — plus a parallel-session harness. Humans reviewed survivors and ran expression / biochemical checks in ordinary lab strains. Stanford bioengineer Stanley Qi told Al Jazeera the striking part was recognizing an unusual pattern and chasing it as a full research question in ~21 hours.

Amodei posted that the company suspects the “molecular machine” could represent a new gene-editing mechanism, and that AI is only at the “very beginning” of this kind of discovery. That is a CEO hypothesis on top of a preprint — keep it labeled as such.

So what

Sep 22 was cheaper Opus with a tighter cage. Sep 23 was malware voting across four LLMs. Sep 24’s lead is different: an AI agent stack that noticed a molecular anomaly in raw DNA and filed a candidate humans are now testing. That is closer to “science lab teammate” than “chatbot with a scoreboard.”

Believe the preprint’s process numbers; do not believe “next CRISPR” until someone shows what ART does. Watch whether other labs reproduce the agent-mining loop on public databases — and whether the next ART-class find comes with a function, not just a pretty repeat array.
