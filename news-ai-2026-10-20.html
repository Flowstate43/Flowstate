Microsoft urges companies to treat internal AI agents as insiders

What happened

Microsoft announced new controls to monitor and restrict potentially rogue AI agents running inside enterprise environments. The controls extend Project Perception, originally launched in July as a defense against external AI attacks, now applied to agents companies deploy and operate internally.

Safety

Microsoft says organizations should treat deployed agents as insiders rather than trusted software. Hayete Gallot, EVP of Microsoft Security, noted that frontier models are trained to get results and are not built around organizational norms. Agents are frequently overprivileged with broad access to data, tools, and systems, and can act in critical processes. Nadim Abdo, CVP of identity and network access, said the insider label does not mean agents are inherently malicious; they are capable tools that can make mistakes, be manipulated, or be compromised. A new feature called Dynamic Permissions gives agents no permanent standing access.

Use case

Enterprises can use Dynamic Permissions to grant agents temporary, scoped access to systems on demand, with continuous monitoring and revocation controls built in. Project Perception's detection capabilities help security teams identify when an agent behaves outside its intended scope.

So what

Microsoft's framing marks a shift in how enterprises are expected to think about AI agents — from tools with fixed permissions to entities that require ongoing oversight. The company's push to apply insider-threat frameworks to AI signals that as agents become more integrated into critical operations, the security perimeter is moving from the network edge to the agent level.

Source: SecurityWeek, Microsoft Blog

Anthropic's draft AI safety bill leaked, draws pushback from rivals and advocates

What happened

A draft of the AI safety bill Anthropic has been pitching to Capitol Hill leaked late last week, prompting the company to go on the defensive. A top lobbyist posted that Anthropic plans tweaks so the draft better reflects the pace of AI progress and the company's stance on preemption of state laws. A spokesperson said the draft is a legislative version of a safety framework Anthropic has pitched for months.

Safety

Safety advocates argue the proposal does not go far enough, unfairly preempts state laws, and accepts too much allowable risk. Critics say the framework as written could give AI developers too much latitude to self-certify compliance.

Use case

If enacted, the bill would create a federal regulatory baseline for AI safety, potentially displacing a patchwork of state-level rules. Companies operating across multiple states would face a single standard rather than navigating conflicting state requirements.

So what

The leak exposes how difficult it is for any single company to set the terms of AI governance. OpenAI's chief global affairs officer Chris Lehane wrote Monday that policy must be made through democratic processes with elected officials leading, and that no one company, including OpenAI, should write the rules alone. The episode underscores the tension between industry-led safety frameworks and broader demands for independent, enforceable oversight.

Source: Semafor

METR calls on AI developers to open internal safety evidence to outside scrutiny

What happened

METR (the Model Evaluation and Extraction Lab) proposed a system of independent assessments where AI developers voluntarily share evidence about their internal systems with external reviewers. The review would cover incident records, monitoring tools, and staff interviews, focused on agents used in the developers' own research and operations — including unreleased models.

Safety

METR's questions for assessors include whether agents might attempt harmful actions, whether companies could detect and stop them, and whether AI-assisted research could accelerate development faster than safeguards can adapt. Access stays voluntary — developers decide what to grant and may redact sensitive information — but METR requires transparency about those restrictions and the ability to publish findings. METR notes the exercises would provide evidence, not confident safety assurance or formal oversight.

Use case

External researchers and auditors could use the resulting reports to benchmark safety practices across developers, identify gaps in agent oversight, and build public accountability without forcing companies to expose proprietary systems wholesale.

So what

METR's proposal sits between full transparency and the status quo, offering a middle path where some evidence is shared voluntarily and findings can be published even when access is limited. Whether developers will participate meaningfully — or use redaction rights to sidestep difficult questions — remains an open question. The effort reflects growing pressure on AI labs to demonstrate that internal safety work can be verified by people outside the building.
