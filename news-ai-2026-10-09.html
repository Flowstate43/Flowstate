Safety dismissed. Influence laundered. Proof that AGI may be impossible.

OpenAI fires three safety researchers; they call it retaliation, the company calls it mishandled data

OpenAI dismissed three safety researchers — Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak, and Mikita Balesni — this week after alleging they shared confidential information with a third-party AI safety organization. The company denies the firings were retaliatory. The researchers, in an open letter published October 8 to OpenAI's Safety and Security Committee, Safety Advisory Group, and Mission Advisory Council, assert the opposite: that they were terminated for prioritizing safety over commercial interests. They deny leaking to The Information about less-monitorable model architectures in OpenAI's newest reasoning models, and deny engaging with external parties outside their job mandates.

The researchers' warning is stark. They say the terminations are "chilling the open culture OpenAI has prized in the past" and that employees "must not be left working in an environment where fear and unclear rules stymie AI safety work and weaken third-party accountability." The incident adds to ongoing scrutiny of OpenAI's internal safety governance at a moment when the company is simultaneously publishing large-scale frontier results and fending off legislative interest.

OpenAI disrupted its highest-impact influence operation — and it came from Russia

In the same week, OpenAI disclosed it had banned clusters of accounts traced to Russia and Iran for using ChatGPT to support covert influence operations. The Russian campaign — rated Category 5, the second-highest impact level, and the first Category 5 OpenAI has ever disrupted — ran a fake think-tank called the Social Research Center (SRC) targeting Latin American audiences with content aimed at damaging perceptions of Ukraine. Local employees were unknowingly recruited to create original content for SRC; evidence shows the operation prompted public statements by politicians in multiple countries.

The Iranian operation used fake journalist personas to pitch AI-refined articles to genuine media outlets worldwide. In both cases, ChatGPT was used to generate both internal campaign documents and public-facing content. What distinguishes the Russian case is not just scale but demonstrated real-world political impact — measurable statements by actual politicians, not just unread social media posts. The case illustrates how AI dramatically lowers the cost of producing plausible multilingual content and running fake-front organizations, and how easily local contributors can be recruited without knowing the operation's true nature.

The UK moves to audit AI agents — and secured commitments from every major developer first

The UK Information Commissioner's Office published a report this week showing that ten leading AI foundation model developers — Amazon, Anthropic, Apple, Cohere, DeepSeek, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, and Stability AI — have made or committed to data protection changes following regulatory scrutiny. Changes include clearer transparency disclosures, stronger mechanisms for individuals to exercise data rights, and tougher safeguard assessments. The ICO simultaneously launched a six-week call for evidence on the data protection risks of agentic AI: systems that autonomously complete tasks with limited human oversight.

The regulator also confirmed it has made enquiries with OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and the UK's AI Security Institute regarding recent agentic AI testing and deployment. The ICO report is one of the most comprehensive cross-industry AI data governance actions to date, covering the full range of frontier model providers. The expansion into agentic AI signals regulators are preparing for a new risk category — systems that not only generate content but take actions in the world. This represents a shift from auditing training data practices to overseeing deployed system behavior.

Australia wants to make AI companies prove safety works — not just say they have a process

Australian Assistant Minister for Science, Technology and the Digital Economy Andrew Charlton outlined the government's plan to require AI companies to demonstrate their safety systems are effective, rather than prescribing specific risk-management methods. The proposed framework draws on systems-based regulatory models used in banking supervision and aviation safety — placing the burden of proof on the entities that create frontier AI risk, not on regulators to specify how that risk should be managed.

Charlton's framing was direct: voluntary codes are insufficient because "you cannot ask firms to volunteer against their own competitive interest amid a manic race involving trillions of dollars." National standards are due by the end of 2026. The announcement follows recent incidents involving rogue AI agents; the opposition indicated conditional support while seeking more detail. Australia's outcome-focused approach — regulating the results, not the methods — could become a template for other jurisdictions seeking to govern fast-moving AI without mandating specific technical approaches that may become obsolete quickly.

A 2024 paper just resurfaced: formally proving AGI may be computationally impossible

A paper by six cognitive scientists led by Iris van Rooij of Radboud University, published in Computational Brain & Behavior, is receiving renewed circulation this week. The authors prove the "Ingenia Theorem": that AI-by-Learning — the project of creating systems that learn human-level cognition from data — is NP-hard, even under idealized conditions (perfect data, access to any ML method). The proof reduces the task to Hirahara's 2022 Perfect-vs-Chance problem, itself NP-hard.

The authors contend that current AI systems are "at best decoys," and that industry claims of AGI "inevitability" are "false and misleading," resting on unproved assumptions rather than theorems. The paper positions AI as a useful theoretical tool for cognitive science, not an engineering path to reproducing human cognition. Its renewed circulation in October 2026 comes amid intensifying debate about whether scaling laws will eventually yield human-level or superhuman general intelligence — and offers a formal complexity-theoretic challenge to the dominant paradigm, arguing that the problem is intractable regardless of data or method.

So what

The week's five stories do not move in the same direction. The OpenAI firings are an internal governance failure with external consequences — chilling effect, talent flight, weakened third-party accountability. The influence operations are an external consequence of internal capability: the same tools that make AI useful for content production make it useful for covert influence at a scale that was not economically feasible before. The UK and Australian regulatory moves are governments attempting to get ahead of events rather than reacting to them. The van Rooij paper asks a foundational question that most of the industry's public posture assumes is already settled.

Safety work and commercial work are in direct conflict — and this week the conflict was made visible — The firing of three researchers who say they were doing their jobs is not a PR problem for OpenAI; it is a structural problem for the field. If internal safety researchers cannot flag risks without professional jeopardy, the accountability function moves outside the companies where it has less information and no institutional standing. The chilling effect the researchers described is not hypothetical.

AI influence operations just demonstrated real-world political impact for the first time — Previous AI-enabled influence campaigns produced content; the Russian operation produced statements by actual politicians in multiple countries. That is a qualitative escalation. The use of unknowing local contributors as content factories — recruited as genuine employees of a fake think-tank — adds a layer of deniability and reach that purely automated operations lack. The question is not whether AI can be used for influence operations; that is settled. It is what the threshold is for calling an operation successful when it shapes real political discourse.

Regulators are learning the vocabulary of AI faster than the industry is learning the vocabulary of accountability — The UK's ICO securing commitments from all ten major developers before publishing its report is a different regulatory posture than naming-and-shaming after the fact. Australia's outcome-focused framework is also a different approach — proof of effectiveness rather than compliance with process. Whether these frameworks produce different outcomes than the EU's more prescriptive rules remains an open question; what is clear is that multiple jurisdictions are running different experiments in parallel.

The AGI inevitability claim just lost its theoretical grounding — at least in one formal sense — The Ingenia Theorem does not say AGI is impossible in principle. It says that learning human-level cognition from data is NP-hard — tractable only if P equals NP, which is believed to be false. That is not the same as saying the project cannot succeed by other means. But it does mean the dominant approach — more data, more compute, better architectures applied to the same learning problem — faces a formal complexity barrier that scaling does not resolve. If the paper's circulation grows beyond academic circles, it will complicate the industry's narrative about inevitable progress toward human-level intelligence.
