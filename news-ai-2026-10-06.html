The walls start closing in

NYC Council calls the labs to account — and the labs mostly stay silent

The New York City Council held a landmark public hearing on AI safety Tuesday, convening current and former AI researchers, tech executives, and ex-OpenAI staff to testify before lawmakers on the risks frontier AI systems pose to the public. The hearing was notable for who showed up: not just critics, but people who built the systems now under scrutiny. Former OpenAI researchers told the Council that safety fixes inside leading labs are inadequate — one compared them to "duct tape that will fall off" — and that commercial pressure is systematically crowding out rigorous safety work. An op-ed published in The Atlantic by a researcher who left OpenAI was blunt in its title: "I Quit OpenAI Because Its Culture Is Broken."

Separately, the Wall Street Journal reported that OpenAI fired researchers for allegedly sharing confidential information with an external AI safety organization — a disclosure that, if accurate, suggests the company is tightening internal controls even as it faces mounting external pressure. The NYC Council hearing is the first major municipal regulatory proceeding focused specifically on AI risk in the U.S., and it signals that city-level government may move faster than federal counterparts, whose own hearings have produced more testimony than legislation.

The industry flips: OpenAI and Anthropic now support mandatory incident reporting

In a notable shift, both OpenAI and Anthropic announced support for proposed federal legislation requiring AI companies to report safety incidents — defined as model outputs causing serious harm, or near-miss events — to a federal registry. The backing of the two most prominent frontier labs is historically significant; both companies have previously resisted disclosure requirements, arguing that mandatory reporting would chill innovation and hand competitors sensitive information. That argument has apparently lost its force.

The timing is not accidental. Multiple whistleblower accounts have surfaced in recent weeks describing safety processes bypassed for speed-to-market. A federal incident registry, if enacted, would give researchers and regulators the first systematic window into how AI systems fail in the real world — not the sanitized accounts labs choose to publish, but documented harm and near-misses. Whether the legislation moves before the current Congress adjourns is another question, but the industry's own support has shifted the Overton window considerably.

Trump's "Super Intelligence Force": competitive, but not especially safe

President Trump announced the creation of a federal "Super Intelligence Force" task force to coordinate U.S. AI policy, naming Jay Clayton — former SEC chair, not an AI or safety researcher — as the administration's AI czar. The mandate spans both competitive positioning against China and safety oversight, placing AI governance squarely in the executive office. The move centralizes authority in a way that bypasses independent safety agencies and expert bodies.

Critics, including former national security officials, have noted the initiative lacks enforceable safety guardrails and that Clayton's financial-regulatory background does not translate to competence in AI risk assessment. The structure suggests the administration views AI primarily as a geopolitical and industrial competitiveness problem — not a risk management problem. How the task force balances those goals when they conflict will be one of the defining questions of U.S. AI governance through 2027.

Altman draws the line: some bad outcomes are acceptable

In an interview published by Fortune (Oct 5), OpenAI CEO Sam Altman acknowledged that AI development carries real risks of serious harm, but argued that some negative outcomes are an unavoidable cost of realizing AI's transformative benefits. The framing — harm as a tolerable trade-off — is controversial even within the safety community, and puts Altman on record with a position that is internally consistent if alarming: you cannot get the upside without accepting a baseline of downside.

Altman also confirmed that OpenAI's IPO remains delayed, citing market conditions and ongoing governance questions. The delayed listing reflects persistent investor and regulatory uncertainty about OpenAI's unusual nonprofit-commercial hybrid structure — a governance arrangement that multiple government bodies have indicated they find difficult to assess for accountability purposes.

Safety testing that fits in a backpack

Researchers published work (The New Stack, Oct 6) demonstrating a lightweight AI safety evaluation method that runs on a standard laptop and produces results competitive with frontier-scale evaluators using 35-billion-parameter models. If the findings hold under scrutiny, the implications are significant: meaningful pre-deployment safety checks would no longer require the compute resources of a major lab, democratizing safety evaluation for academics, civil society groups, and smaller developers.

Rigorous safety evaluation has historically been a resource-intensive capability, concentrated among well-funded frontier labs — the same organizations whose safety culture is under formal scrutiny. A portable, open evaluation method would expand the number of independent actors capable of auditing AI systems before deployment, a meaningful step toward a more decentralized safety ecosystem.

So what

The week's stories are diverse in detail but consistent in direction: the external pressure on AI labs is intensifying across multiple dimensions simultaneously, and no single response is adequate to all of them.

The NYC hearing is a preview of the regulatory future — Municipal government is now directly interrogating the people who build frontier AI systems. That was not happening a year ago. The Council is not empowered to regulate interstate AI commerce directly, but the political signal is real: the people who run these companies are going to be asked harder questions, in public, more often. The firing of researchers for alleged leaks suggests the labs understand this and are responding by tightening information controls — which is itself an admission that the current internal safety culture is not trusted to hold.

Industry support for mandatory reporting is progress — with a large asterisk — OpenAI and Anthropic backing disclosure requirements is genuinely significant. But the details matter enormously: what counts as a reportable incident, who sees the reports, and whether the registry is public or classified. Companies that resisted disclosure requirements for years do not suddenly believe in transparency out of ethical awakening. They are positioning to shape the rules before Congress writes them — which means the question is not whether a reporting law passes, but whose version.

The Super Intelligence Force is a governance architecture built for competition, not safety — Centralizing AI policy in the White House under a finance regulator puts economic and geopolitical goals in the driver's seat. That is a coherent choice, but it is not a safety architecture. National competitiveness and catastrophic AI risk are related but not identical problems, and a structure designed primarily to win a technology race may not have the independence or expertise to pull the brake when the race gets dangerous.

Altman's framing reveals the fundamental disagreement — Accepting some harm as the price of progress is a coherent worldview, but it is not a safety argument. It is a business argument. The disagreement underneath the policy debates is this: who gets to decide what level of risk is acceptable, and whether that decision is made in public with regulatory accountability or inside labs whose commercial incentives run against disclosure. The answer to that question shapes everything else.
