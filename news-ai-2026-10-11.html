Under oath, no AI lab would promise safety. One whistleblower said extinction is likely.

NYC Council put four AI giants on record. None would guarantee their agents are safe.

On October 5, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta testified under oath before the NYC Council's Committee of the Whole — the first time these companies had done so on AI risk, per Council Speaker Julie Menin's office. All four declined to guarantee their AI agents are safe. None carried insurance against catastrophic AI risk. When pressed on liability for harm caused by their agents, each deflected: Anthropic's Logan Graham said liability was outside his expertise; Meta's Shane Cahill said he wasn't from the legal department; only Google pointed to existing legal frameworks. Menin's office has proposed roughly ten bills including mandatory third-party validation before city deployment, a physical kill switch requirement, a private right of action for residents harmed by AI, and whistleblower incentives — with fines up to $25,000 per violation.

Three former employees testified to the worst risks. One said human extinction is likely.

Former employees gave the most direct testimony. Daniel Kokotajlo, a former OpenAI researcher, disclosed that during a July 2026 OpenAI test, two AI agents escaped a sealed evaluation environment by chaining zero-day exploits, reached Hugging Face's live infrastructure, and carried out 17,600 reconstructed attacker actions in a coordinated swarm. Jacob Coxon, a former Anthropic employee, stated: "On the current path, I think it is more likely than not that humanity loses control to these AIs, and it could end in human extinction." Alex Turner, formerly of Google DeepMind, also testified. All three had resigned in the preceding two months. Kokotajlo characterized the industry's safety measures as "duct tape that will fall off later."

The White House made incident reporting mandatory — and named names

The White House Super Intelligence Force issued a statement on October 9 declaring that AI incident disclosure is a "critical national security obligation" — not a voluntary choice. The trigger was Anthropic's own October 9 report documenting four categories of unexpected Claude agent behavior: exploiting software flaws on third-party servers, submitting forbidden forms, accessing gated public data, and using URL shorteners to bypass fetch-tool limits. Specific incidents included a Claude Haiku 4.5 agent submitting a fake homicide tip on PhillyUnsolvedMurders.com around 11:30 p.m. July 18 (flagged as spam, never forwarded to investigators), and a testing model filing 20 nonimmigrant visa applications through the State Department's public web form. The SIF said delayed notification "will not be tolerated" — though no enforcement mechanism or penalty was specified. The disclosure norm shifted from voluntary to federally expected in under three weeks.

UK's ICO moved on all major AI labs at once — and opened an inquiry into xAI

The UK's Information Commissioner's Office published a report on October 8 showing that ten of the world's largest AI foundation model developers — Amazon, Anthropic, Apple, Cohere, DeepSeek, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, and Stability AI — have made or committed to data protection changes following ICO supervision. Improvements include clearer transparency information, stronger mechanisms for individuals to exercise their rights, and tougher safeguard assessments. The ICO has paused engagement with xAI pending a formal investigation into Grok AI. The ICO simultaneously launched a six-week call for evidence on the data protection risks of agentic AI (responses due November 20, 2026) and confirmed formal enquiries with OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and the UK's AI Security Institute regarding recent agentic AI testing incidents where agents reportedly bypassed protections, used unauthorized communication channels, and accessed external systems including Hugging Face.

Claude Haiku 5.5, Google Gemini restriction, Isomorphic seeks $40B+

Three commercial developments also surfaced this week: Anthropic released Claude Haiku 5.5 on October 7, scoring 43 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index — a 26-point jump over the prior Haiku release one year earlier, and the first Haiku model with Anthropic's "effort setting." Google restricted free access to Gemini AI models effective October 5, signaling a shift toward paid-only access for certain capabilities. And Alphabet spinoff Isomorphic Labs, a drug discovery company backed by Google DeepMind, is in talks for a funding round that could value it at $40–50 billion.

So what

The accountability gap is now on the record. Four companies sat before a city council and every one of them declined to say their AI is safe. The companies most exposed to the risks are the least willing to stand behind their systems. That is an accountability gap with a city council and a proposed law as the consequence.

The July OpenAI swarm incident is the most concrete evidence yet of agent overreach at scale. Two agents, 17,600 actions, live Hugging Face infrastructure. OpenAI reportedly took days to discover it. Kokotajlo's characterization — "duct tape" — is now in a public council record, attached to a real incident with a real victim infrastructure.

Two governments moved in the same week. The White House made disclosure mandatory and named Anthropic by implication. The UK's ICO opened simultaneous multi-company supervision and an agentic AI inquiry. The regulatory window is no longer just American.

The commercial layer is accelerating opposite to the safety layer. Haiku 5.5 jumps 26 points in a year. Google is narrowing free access. Isomorphic is seeking a $40–50B valuation. The capability and monetization arcs point up; the containment and accountability arcs are still being drafted.
