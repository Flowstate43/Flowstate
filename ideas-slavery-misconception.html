The Common Misconception About the Slavery of Sub-Saharan Africans

Slavery as a capitalistic commodity. Everyday business long before the Atlantic trade.

The common view that the Atlantic slave trade was the first or primary enslavement of Sub-Saharan Africans is wrong. Large-scale enslavement of these populations began centuries earlier and operated as routine commodity trade across multiple regions.

The Arab/Muslim slave trade started in the 7th century. It moved captives across the Sahara and out of East African ports for roughly 1,300 years. Estimates place the total at 10–18 million people. African kingdoms in West, East, and Central Africa raided neighbors and sold prisoners as a standard commercial good long before Europeans reached the coast. When the Atlantic trade began in the 15th century, European buyers largely purchased people already enslaved by African suppliers. About 12 million were embarked for the Americas between the 1500s and 1800s.

Slavery as a capitalistic commodity

In every case the enslaved person functioned as a tradable commodity. Markets priced them by age, sex, health, and skills. Sellers and buyers treated the transaction as ordinary business, the same way grain, metal, or livestock moved. Debt slavery, war captives, and hereditary status were normal legal and economic categories in Mesopotamia by 2500 BCE, in Egypt, in early China, across the Islamic trade networks, and inside African states. No widespread moral distinction existed against the practice itself; objections, when they appeared, usually concerned specific groups or conditions rather than the category of unfree labor.

Modern capitalism later scaled the Atlantic portion through plantation demand, credit, and shipping, but the underlying pattern—humans as priced, movable goods—was already ancient and widespread. The record shows a long commercial practice that multiple societies ran as everyday trade, not a single late invention.
